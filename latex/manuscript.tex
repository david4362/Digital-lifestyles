\begin{abstract}
Digitalization has been portrayed as holding a potential to reduce household carbon footprints by substituting for travel and material consumption, or increase them by facilitating and stimulating additional consumption. Detailed evidence on the net relationship on the individual-level remains scarce. We link survey measures of digital practices among Swedish adults to annual consumption-based greenhouse-gas emissions estimated from bank transactions and to sociodemographic registers. The complete-case analysis includes 4,250 participants. Our revised digital lifestyle index combines 11 device-use and digital-activity frequencies. After adjustment for age, gender, income, education, household composition, and urbanity, a one-standard-deviation higher digital lifestyle index is associated with 204 kg CO\textsubscript{2}e more per year (95\% CI: 141--267), or 4.5\% of mean annual emissions. The gradient is carried by communicative practices---calls and social media---whereas solitary screen practices such as streaming and gaming show null or negative associations. It is distributed across multiple consumption categories, is accompanied by 3.1\% greater observed expenditure, and shows no gradient in emissions per krona spent. Contrary to a digital-substitution account, transport emissions and both at-home and out-of-home leisure are higher among more digitally engaged participants. Device-assisted mobile screen time is only weakly
correlated with the broader index and has no independent association with emissions. The results identify digital engagement as a marker of consumption volume within socially active lifestyles rather than evidence that additional screen time itself causes emissions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Digital technologies are embedded in everyday life: in how people work,
communicate, shop, and spend their leisure time. The environmental consequences
of this pervasive digitalization are not straightforward. A video meeting can
replace a trip to another city, online shopping can replace several visits to
stores, and gaming with friends can replace an evening out. These practices can
reduce travel, but they also save time and make consumption activities easier to fit into already busy lives. The same technologies can lower the effort required to search for, advertise, purchase, and get delivery of goods and services, while creating new activities rather than replacing existing ones. Digitalization can therefore reduce the environmental burden of a particular task without reducing the overall level of consumption associated with a person's way of life.

Research on the environmental effects of information and communication
technologies (ICT) distinguishes the direct footprint of devices and digital
infrastructure from wider changes in production and consumption. Reviews find
that these indirect and higher-order effects are difficult to delimit and can
either offset or amplify direct efficiency gains \citep{horner2016,bieser2018,
pohl2019}. At the macro level, efficiency and sectoral change may reduce energy
requirements, while the production of ICT equipment, productivity-led growth,
and rebound effects work in the opposite direction \citep{lange2020}. The net
effect consequently depends on how technologies are adopted and how people use
the time, money, and opportunities they create.

Evidence from specific applications illustrates this ambiguity. The long-
standing expectation that telecommunications substitute for physical travel
has been qualified by evidence that communication and mobility can also
complement one another \citep{mokhtarian2002,andreev2010,aguilera2012}. A
systematic review of teleworking found that reduced commuting can save energy,
but that wider-boundary studies generally find smaller gains once home energy
use and non-work travel are considered \citep{hook2020}. Similarly, whether
online shopping has lower emissions than store-based shopping depends on
delivery organization, travel mode, trip chaining, basket size, and returns
\citep{wiese2012,shahmohammadi2020}. These studies show why the footprint of an
isolated digital service cannot establish whether people whose everyday lives
are more extensively mediated by digital tools consume less overall.

An everyday-life perspective offers a different starting point. Digital tools
are not merely substitutes for analogue equivalents; they become woven into
bundles of work, social relations, entertainment, mobility, and consumption
\citep{ropke2012}. A systematic review of indirect ICT effects identified
consumer behaviour, social practices, and the dynamic consequences of adoption
as important research gaps \citep{bieser2018}. This perspective also changes the
interpretation of individual differences. Frequent calls, information searches,
or social-media use may facilitate consumption, but they may also identify
people who are more socially active, professionally connected, affluent, or
otherwise engaged in a larger number of activities that digital tools enable. Digital engagement may thus operate as a medium within a consumption-intensive lifestyle rather than as an independent cause of that lifestyle.

Some digital practices nevertheless provide plausible pathways to additional
consumption. Social-media platforms combine advertising, peer comparison, and
self-presentation. Existing studies relate social-media use to social
comparison and lower self-evaluations \citep{vogel2014,verduyn2017}, to envy and
conspicuous consumption \citep{taylor2016}, and to impulse fashion purchases
\citep{djafarova2021}. This literature makes a demand-stimulation mechanism
plausible, but much of the evidence concerns self-reported attitudes, purchase
intentions, or particular products. It does not establish whether social-media
engagement predicts total observed consumption; reverse causation and common lifestyle determinants remain equally possible.

Household carbon footprints rise strongly with affluence, making socioeconomic
adjustment essential when studying digital lifestyles \citep{ivanova2016,
oswald2020,wiedmann2020}. At the same time, conventional expenditure surveys and
application-specific life-cycle studies offer limited visibility into the
breadth of an individual's consumption. Financial-transaction data can provide
a more comprehensive, behaviorally observed account of expenditure and its
associated emissions \citep{andersson2023}. Linking such data to survey measures
of digital practices and administrative registers creates an opportunity to
study digital engagement as part of an observed consumption system rather than
as an isolated technology use case.

We use linked survey, bank-transaction, and register data from Swedish adults to
ask three questions. First, how is more frequent use of common digital practices
associated with total consumption-based greenhouse-gas emissions after
socioeconomic adjustment? Second, is the gradient concentrated in transport,
goods commonly purchased online, or digital services, or is it distributed
across consumption categories? Third, extending the same logic in exploratory
analyses developed after preregistration, are more digitally engaged
participants more or less socially active than others---as indicated by
measured mobile screen time and reported leisure activities---and does the
answer support substitution away from out-of-home consumption?
Our primary exposure is a transparent composite of heterogeneous digital practices, not a measure of a single latent trait or of total time online. The analyses estimate conditional cross-sectional associations and cannot identify the causal effect of increasing digital use. Their contribution is instead to test whether digitally engaged lifestyles appear dematerialized when broad consumption is observed at the individual level.

\section{Data}

\subsection{Sample}
We use a dataset that links surveys, bank transactions, user-profile information, and Swedish administrative registers at the individual level. Participants connected their bank account(s) to a research application, allowing historical transactions to be observed and categorized into consumption categories. The baseline survey measured device use and digital activities; a subsequent endline survey collected device-assisted mobile screen time and activity frequencies. All analyses of identifiable register-linked data were conducted in University of Gothenburg's Trusted Research Environment.

The baseline survey contains 12,153 unique participants. Of these participants,
11,172 satisfy the digital-battery response rule described below. We define an
observed transaction month as one in which non-excluded spending exceeds SEK
3,000 and require at least three such months during the 12 months preceding the
baseline survey. This restriction leaves 4,392 participants. Linkage to the
control variables leaves 4,251 participants, of whom 4,250 have complete data
for the fully adjusted model. We freeze this complete-case sample for all steps
of the main model sequence so that changes between specifications do not reflect
changes in sample composition.

\subsection{Consumption-based carbon footprints}
Transactions are assigned to consumption categories and converted to kilograms of carbon-dioxide
equivalents (CO\textsubscript{2}e) using category-specific emissions factors \citep{andersson2020,andersson2023}.
We aggregate transactions by participant, month, and category. For each
participant--category combination observed in at least three valid months,
annual emissions are calculated as the sum over observed months divided by the
number of observed months and multiplied by 12. Annual emissions are winsorized
at the category-specific 99th percentile before categories are summed. The
primary outcome is total annual consumption-based CO\textsubscript{2}e.

We decompose emissions into theoretically motivated groups. Transport comprises fuel, vehicle maintenance and rental, public and
other ground transport, aviation, and ferry travel. The e-commerce-intensive
goods group comprises clothing, electronics, books, toys, sports goods, other
shopping, and home-and-garden goods; it denotes categories commonly purchased
online rather than transactions known to have occurred online. Internet and
telecommunications form the digital-services category. Insurance is used as a
low-channel placebo. We also retain mutually exclusive leaf categories to show
where the total gradient is distributed. Spending outcomes are constructed from
the corresponding transaction amounts using the same observation window and
annualization rule.

\subsection{Digital lifestyle intensity}
The baseline survey asks how frequently participants used five devices
(computer, smartphone, television, game console, and tablet) and performed
eight digital activities (streaming, phone or video calls, social media,
information search, gaming, digital reading, e-commerce, and online selling)
during the preceding three months. All items use a six-point response scale
ranging from no use to several times per hour.

The pre-registration specified an equally weighted index containing all 13
items. After registration, we recognized that e-commerce and online selling
measure the purchase channel itself and are therefore conceptually too proximal
to transaction-observed consumption outcomes. We treat their inclusion as a
design mistake rather than redefine the registered specification
retrospectively. The substantively preferred index consequently excludes these
two items and contains 11 items; the registered 13-item index is retained as a
sensitivity analysis.

Each item is standardized using its mean and standard deviation in the frozen
analysis sample. The index is the unweighted mean of the answered standardized
items and is itself re-standardized to mean zero and standard deviation one.
Following the pre-registered tolerance of up to three missing items, the revised
index requires at least eight of 11 responses. The revised index has Cronbach's $\alpha=0.56$. We interpret it as a formative composite of heterogeneous digital practices rather than as a reflective scale whose items are expected to be interchangeable.

At endline, respondents were instructed to read their average daily mobile
screen time for the previous week from iOS Screen Time or Android Digital
Wellbeing and transcribe hours and minutes. Reports above 18 hours per day are
treated as implausible. There are 2,200 valid reports, of which 2,082 can be
linked to a valid revised index. We regress measured hours on the standardized
index to obtain an interpretive anchor. The association is weak
($b=0.338$ hours per index SD, $R^2=0.018$), so kilograms per index SD is our
primary scale. Values per anchored hour are secondary and should not be read as
effects of an additional measured screen-time hour. Missing screen-time reports
do not exclude participants from analyses using the baseline index.

\subsection{Register variables}
The control variables comprise of demographic, application-profile, transaction, and register information. It includes age; gender; log income; log disposable register income; log mean monthly bank inflows;
educational attainment in five levels; household size; an indicator for
children; log population density in the participant's DeSO area; and an
indicator for residence in Stockholm, Gothenburg, or Malm\"o. One participant
is excluded because bank-income data are missing. Exact age enters linearly in
the main specification; a four-degree-of-freedom natural spline assesses
sensitivity to functional form.

\section{Empirical strategy}

\subsection{Net gradient}
We estimate cross-sectional ordinary least-squares models of the form
\begin{equation}
	Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \boldsymbol{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i,
	\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
where $Y_i$ is annual consumption-based CO\textsubscript{2}e, $D_i$ is the
standardized digital lifestyle index, and $\boldsymbol{X}_i$ is the adjustment
set. The coefficient $\beta$ is reported as kilograms of annual
CO\textsubscript{2}e per index standard deviation. We use HC3
heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors and two-sided tests with 95\%
confidence intervals.

Controls are introduced sequentially on the same complete-case sample: M0 is
bivariate; M1 adds gender and age; M2 adds the three income measures and
education; M3 adds household size and children; M4 adds log population density;
and M5 adds major-city residence. M5 is the primary adjusted specification.
These models estimate conditional gradients, not causal effects: digital
practices are not randomly assigned, and unmeasured preferences, employment,
or life circumstances may influence both digital behavior and consumption.

\subsection{Decomposition}
We replace total emissions in Equation~\ref{eq:main} with each grouped and leaf
category while retaining the M5 adjustment set. The transport coefficient tests
the proposed demobilization margin; e-commerce-intensive goods and digital
services capture material and direct digital channels. Because grouped and leaf
categories overlap, grouped estimates are interpreted separately rather than
summed. The mutually exclusive leaf estimates provide the additive description
of the total gradient. Insurance serves as a low-channel falsification outcome,
although low mean emissions limit the precision of formal equivalence tests.

To examine whether the emissions gradient coincides with greater consumption,
we additionally estimate M5 models for annual emission-relevant spending and
total observed spending. These spending analyses are descriptive and are not
interpreted as causal mediation.

\subsection{Mechanism}
The pre-registered mechanism proposed that digital engagement substitutes for
out-of-home activity and thereby reduces transport. The endline survey records
12-month frequencies on seven-point scales for seven out-of-home activities
(library visits, sports and cultural events, hosting dinner guests, dancing,
exercise, and time in nature) and five primarily at-home activities
(audiobooks, music, books, films, and television series). Because hosting dinner
guests, exercise, and time in nature do not invariably entail travel or paid
consumption, we refer to the first measure more precisely as the
out-of-home/social-activity composite. We form unweighted means of the available
items within each domain.

We first regress each activity and both composites on the digital index and M5
controls. We then estimate the transport and total-emissions gradients on one
common endline subsample: without the leisure composites, adding out-of-home
leisure, and adding both composites. The percentage attenuation of $\beta$ is
reported as the share of the gradient statistically accounted for by these
measures. Because exposure, leisure, and emissions are not jointly randomized
or temporally isolated, this is a descriptive mechanism check rather than a
causal mediation analysis.

\subsection{Heterogeneity}
We report M5-adjusted index gradients by pre-specified age group
(18--29, 30--44, 45--64, and 65+), gender, and major-city residence. Formal
tests add the corresponding index-by-group interaction to M5; differences
between subgroup point estimates are not inferred from separate confidence
intervals. Income-quintile patterns are exploratory.

\subsection{Design caveats}
The baseline frequency battery precedes the endline screen-time report, while
transaction outcomes are constructed from the 12 months preceding the baseline
survey. The anchor therefore maps a broader, earlier digital-practice composite
onto a one-week measure of later mobile screen time; it does not establish that
the two constructs are equivalent. We accordingly separate the main digital
lifestyle analysis from models using measured screen time itself.

Observed spending may also depend on how completely participants connected
their bank accounts. We assess this by adding account-count controls, estimating
models within account-count strata, normalizing spending and emissions by
register income, and restricting the sample to participants whose observed
spending is within pre-defined proportions of register income. Further
sensitivity analyses use the registered 13-item index, the index excluding
calls, device and activity subindices, a first principal component, a nonlinear
age adjustment, DeSO fixed effects, stricter transaction coverage, alternative
housing and life-stage controls, and the subsample with usable mobile
screen-time reports. These checks
probe measurement and specification dependence but do not remove the
cross-sectional design's fundamental risk of residual confounding and reverse
causation.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Screen-time validity}
Among the 2,200 respondents who provided a usable reading from their phone's
screen-time function, mean mobile screen time was 4.39 hours per day (median
3.95; SD 2.41); 51 reports above 18
hours per day were excluded. In the 2,082 respondents with both measures, the
correlation between measured mobile screen time and the digital lifestyle index
was weak ($r=0.134$, $R^2=0.018$). This suggests that the mobile screen time alone cannot be used as a proxy for a broader set of digital practices.

Among the 1,969 people who both provided a usable screen-time reading and had
complete information on all adjustment variables, measured screen time was not
associated with annual emissions ($27$ kg CO\textsubscript{2}e per measured
hour; 95\% CI: $-10$ to $64$; $p=0.148$). When the index and measured screen
time were entered jointly on standardized scales, the index remained associated
with emissions ($233$ kg CO\textsubscript{2}e per SD; 95\% CI: $138$--$329$;
$p<0.001$), whereas measured screen time did not ($29$ kg per SD; 95\% CI:
$-59$ to $117$; $p=0.518$).

\subsection{The net gradient}
In the frozen complete-case sample ($N=4,250$), a one-SD higher revised digital
lifestyle index was associated with $180$ kg CO\textsubscript{2}e higher annual
emissions in the bivariate model (95\% CI: $105$--$254$; 4.0\% of the sample
mean of 4,501 kg). Adjustment for age and gender increased the estimate to
$274$ kg (6.1\%), while adding income and education reduced it to $198$ kg
(4.4\%). The fully adjusted estimate was $204$ kg per SD
(95\% CI: $141$--$267$; $p<0.001$), equivalent to 4.5\% of mean annual
emissions.

Here, ``fully adjusted'' means that the comparison accounts for age, gender,
three measures of income, education, household size, children in the household,
neighbourhood population density, and residence in one of Sweden's three major
cities. Figure~\ref{fig:stepwise} shows how the estimate changes as these
variables are introduced.

Digital engagement was also associated with greater observed expenditure:
SEK 9,690 more total observed annual spending per SD (95\% CI: 6,406--12,975;
$p<0.001$), or 3.1\% of the sample mean. The expenditure and emissions
gradients are two views of the same behavior: emissions per 1,000 SEK spent
showed no adjusted association with the index ($p=0.167$), and comparing
participants at equal spending removed 83\% of the emissions gradient. More
digitally engaged participants thus emitted more because they consumed more,
not because each krona was spent on more carbon-intensive goods.

The registered 13-item index gave a somewhat larger estimate ($237$ kg per SD;
95\% CI: $173$--$301$). Excluding calls reduced the estimate to $141$ kg
(95\% CI: $77$--$204$), but the association remained positive. Estimates were
also positive for the device-use half ($151$ kg), digital-activity half
($190$ kg), and a data-derived summary that gives more weight to practices that
tend to vary together ($207$ kg).

Adding bank-account count reduced the headline estimate by 10.7\%, to $182$ kg
per SD. Restrictions to participants whose observed spending was 25--200\% or
50--150\% of register income yielded estimates of $164$ and $155$ kg,
respectively; both remained positive and statistically distinguishable from
zero.

\subsection{A sociality signature across digital practices}
The item-level results follow a consistent and unanticipated pattern: the
gradient is carried by digital practices that connect people to other people,
not by solitary screen use. Calls had the largest separate gradient ($346$ kg
per SD; Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted $p<0.001$) and remained strongly
associated with emissions conditional on the other ten revised-index items
($309$ kg). Social media was second ($191$ kg separately; $105$ kg
conditionally; 95\% CI: $36$--$174$). By contrast, streaming was negatively
associated with emissions ($-80$ kg separately; $-147$ kg conditionally;
95\% CI: $-211$ to $-83$), and gaming was weakly negative ($-65$ kg; not
significant after multiplicity adjustment).

The category pattern reinforces this contrast. A one-SD higher social-media
frequency predicted more restaurant emissions ($18$ kg; 95\% CI: $11$--$25$),
more transport emissions ($66$ kg; 95\% CI: $32$--$101$), and more emissions
from goods commonly bought online ($13$ kg; 95\% CI: $3$--$24$). Gaming
predicted none of these (restaurants $2$ kg, transport $-6$ kg, goods $4$ kg,
all $p>0.2$) and was associated only with digital-services emissions ($2.1$ kg;
$p<0.001$), consistent with subscription spending. The practices most
associated with the stereotype of a sedentary online life were thus the only
ones compatible with a dematerialized consumption pattern, while communicative
practices marked socially active, consumption-intensive lives. These item
analyses are exploratory and do not identify the direction of influence.

\subsection{Decomposition across consumption categories}
The registration made directional predictions: negative gradients for
out-of-home social and cultural consumption (H2a) and transport (H2b),
positive gradients for goods and digital services (H2c), and a null insurance
placebo tested by equivalence (H2d). The first two predictions were rejected.
Transport emissions increased by $46$ kg CO\textsubscript{2}e per index SD
(95\% CI: $9$--$82$; $p=0.015$), contrary to the predicted demobilization, and
restaurant emissions---the largest component of the registered social and
cultural group---increased by $22$ kg ($p<0.001$) \todo{add the registered
social/cultural group estimate from the next TRE run}. H2c was supported:
emissions from the e-commerce-intensive goods group increased by $28$ kg
(95\% CI: $18$--$39$; $p<0.001$) and internet and telecommunications by
$1.7$ kg (95\% CI: $0.7$--$2.7$; $p<0.001$).

The registered placebo equivalence test (H2d) was inconclusive. The insurance
estimate was small ($0.14$ kg per SD; 90\% CI: $-0.01$ to $0.30$), but the
registered equivalence bound of $\pm1\%$ of the category mean corresponds to
only $\pm0.01$ kg per SD, far below what the data can resolve (minimum
detectable effect $\approx0.26$ kg). The placebo is therefore uninformative at
the registered threshold, although the point estimate equals less than 0.1\%
of the total gradient. In spending terms---not the registered scale, which is
CO\textsubscript{2}e---insurance rose SEK 77 per SD (95\% CI: $-23$ to $177$;
$p=0.13$), or 4.7\% of the category mean: not distinguishable from zero, but
similar in relative size to several non-placebo categories, so the placebo
provides little discriminating power in either unit.

Because category means differ widely, proportional changes describe the
consumption pattern better than absolute kilograms. Relative to category
means, the gradient is steepest in out-of-home and discretionary consumption:
aviation (+10.3\% per index SD), travel (+9.0\%), alcohol (+8.1\%),
restaurants (+7.0\%), and other shopping (+6.5\%), against +4.7\% for clothing
and +3.5\% for fuel (Figure~\ref{fig:co2-decomposition}). Groceries show the
largest absolute gradient ($39$ kg per SD) but a proportional increase
(+5.4\%) close to the overall 4.5\%, and in spending terms groceries rise only
+1.9\% per SD---the absolute figure partly reflects the category's high
emission intensity rather than disproportionate food consumption. The pattern
is therefore broad, strongest in socially embedded discretionary categories,
and not confined to digital services or goods commonly bought online.

The spending gradients show the same profile
(Figure~\ref{fig:sek-decomposition}): travel (+8.9\% of the category mean per
SD), restaurants (+7.0\%), other shopping (+6.4\%), the e-commerce-intensive
goods group (+5.4\%), clothing (+4.6\%), and internet and telecommunications
(+4.1\%), against +1.9\% for groceries and $-2.4$\% for rent. These grouped
and leaf-category estimates overlap and should not be added together.

\subsection{Mechanism: out-of-home leisure}
More digitally engaged participants did not report less out-of-home or social
activity. The adjusted out-of-home/social-activity composite increased by 0.073
points per index SD (95\% CI: 0.037--0.109; $p<0.001$), while the at-home
composite increased by 0.137 points (95\% CI: 0.101--0.172; $p<0.001$).
Positive associations were observed for attending sports events (0.164 points),
attending cultural events (0.088), hosting dinner guests (0.137), and dancing
(0.144), as well as for audiobooks, music, films, and television series.

On the common mechanism subsample ($N=2,392$), the fully adjusted transport gradient was
$62.7$ kg CO\textsubscript{2}e per SD. Adding the out-of-home/social-activity
composite reduced it to $61.6$ kg, and adding both activity composites reduced
it to $56.7$ kg, an attenuation of 9.6\%. For total emissions, the corresponding
estimates were $237.8$, $227.4$, and $213.2$ kg per SD, an attenuation of
10.3\% after both composites. Adjusting instead for reported work-from-home
frequency changed the transport gradient from $62.5$ to $58.9$ kg per SD
($N=2,391$), so telework likewise accounts for little of the association.
These measures therefore accounted for only a
limited share of the observed gradients and did not support substitution away
from out-of-home activity (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}).

\subsection{Heterogeneity}
Adjusted subgroup estimates were positive for participants aged 18--29
($150$ kg per SD), 30--44 ($263$ kg), and 45--64 ($232$ kg). The estimate for
participants aged 65 and older was imprecise ($561$ kg; 95\% CI: $-544$ to
$1,665$). Estimates were $157$ kg among women and $253$ kg among men, but the
index-by-gender interaction was not statistically significant ($p=0.134$).
They were $182$ kg outside the three major cities and $249$ kg within them; the
index-by-city interaction was also not statistically significant ($p=0.323$).
The results therefore provide no clear evidence that the gradient differs by
gender or major-city residence (Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}).

\section{Discussion}

People who used a wider range of digital devices and services more frequently
also spent more and had larger consumption-based carbon footprints when controlling for income and other relevant variables. A one-
standard-deviation difference in the digital-practice composite corresponded to
about SEK 9,700 (3.1\%) more in total observed annual spending and 204 kg
(4.5\%) more annual CO\textsubscript{2}e after adjustment for demographic,
socioeconomic, household, and geographic characteristics.
The emissions difference was spread across
several consumption categories and remained positive under alternative ways of
combining the digital-practice items. Digitalized everyday life therefore did
not appear dematerialized in this sample.

This finding does not mean that spending time online causes people to consume
more. Mobile screen time was only weakly related to the broader digital-practice
composite and was not independently associated with emissions. Moreover, the
individual practices can have different meanings. Frequent phone and video
calls, which showed the largest item-level gradient, may reflect occupational
meetings, private communication, or a generally active social and professional
life. Digital tools may therefore facilitate consumption while also serving as
markers of the activities, resources, and relationships through which
consumption occurs. The cross-sectional data cannot separate these roles.

The results provide little support for substitution at the level of the whole
lifestyle. Participants with more frequent digital practices did not report
less out-of-home or social activity, and their transport emissions were higher,
not lower. Accounting for reported leisure activities explained only a small
part of the transport and total-emissions gradients. This is consistent with
research showing that telecommunications can complement physical mobility and
that estimated energy savings from telework become smaller when home energy and
additional travel are included \citep{mokhtarian2002,hook2020}. Individual
applications may still replace particular journeys or purchases. Our result is
that these substitutions did not add up to a lower-footprint lifestyle among
people who used digital practices more frequently.

The contrasts between individual practices caution against treating
digitalization as one uniform exposure and suggest what the composite actually
measures. The gradient is carried by communicative practices: calls and
social media predicted higher emissions, including in restaurants and
transport, whereas streaming was negatively associated with emissions and
gaming predicted only digital-services spending. Solitary screen practices,
the stereotype of a sedentary online life, were thus the only ones compatible
with dematerialization; digital practices that connect people to other people
marked consumption-intensive lives. Advertising, social comparison, and
self-presentation could contribute to the social-media pattern
\citep{vogel2014,taylor2016,djafarova2021}, but we did not measure these
mechanisms, and the sociality pattern itself was identified after seeing the
data. It should be treated as a hypothesis for confirmation---most directly by
testing whether communication-practice gradients are explained by measured
social activity---rather than as evidence that particular platforms increase
or reduce consumption.

The study extends environmental assessments that compare a digital service with
a specific non-digital alternative. Such comparisons are indispensable, but
their boundaries can miss activities that are added, displaced, or reorganized
elsewhere in everyday life \citep{horner2016,bieser2018,pohl2019}. The
distinction parallels the broader efficiency--sufficiency debate: digital
services can make individual activities more efficient, but whether emissions
fall depends on total consumption volumes, which efficiency alone does not
constrain \citep{lange2020,wiedmann2020}. Linking
surveyed practices to transaction-derived footprints instead shows the broader
consumption context in which digital technologies are used. The approach cannot
identify the footprint caused by a particular technology, but it can test the
more general expectation that digitally intensive ways of living coincide with
lower material consumption. Here, they did not.

Several limitations qualify both the size and interpretation of the gradient.
Unmeasured differences in employment, social activity, preferences, or life
circumstances may affect digital practices and consumption simultaneously. The
frequency questions do not measure duration, purpose, or whether an activity
replaced an analogue alternative. Transactions cover the year before the
baseline survey, while screen time and leisure were measured later. Participants
were also a selected group willing to connect bank accounts, and their connected
accounts may not capture all household spending. Adjustment for account count
and restrictions based on spending relative to register income reduced but did
not remove the association; those restrictions can themselves select people by
real saving, borrowing, or household financial arrangements. None of these
checks turns the estimate into a causal effect.

The policy implication is not that digital services should be discouraged, but
that their climate benefits should not be assumed from technical substitution
alone. Prospective assessments should specify what a service replaces and also
consider whether it saves time, lowers transaction costs, complements travel,
or expands the number of activities people undertake. For research, the next
step is longitudinal evidence that can distinguish digital tools as causes,
mediators, and markers of consumption-intensive lifestyles. For climate
strategy, the present results suggest that digitalization requires the same
attention to overall consumption volume as other forms of efficiency and
convenience.

\section*{Pre-registration}
The original analysis plan was registered on the Open Science Framework before
any member of the research team analyzed the digital-lifestyle exposures in the
real linked data on 3 September 2026
(\href{https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/RE7BS}{doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/RE7BS}). The team
had previously analyzed the transaction outcomes and several covariates for a
separate study, and analysis code for the present study had been developed using
a partly scrambled extract and synthetic endline data. These forms of prior
access were disclosed in the registration. The original registration specified
the net-emissions gradient, category decomposition, leisure mechanism,
heterogeneity analyses, the 13-item digital index, and its mobile-screen-time
anchor.

The principal deviation concerns exposure construction. The registered index
included e-commerce and online selling. We subsequently concluded that this was
a design mistake because these items directly measure participation in purchase
channels while the outcomes are transaction-observed consumption. We therefore
use the 11-item index excluding those two items as the substantively preferred,
post-registration correction and retain the registered 13-item estimate in all
index-sensitivity results. The associated response threshold changes from at
least 10 of 13 to at least eight of 11 items, preserving the registered
tolerance of three missing responses. We also make the index-SD scale primary,
rather than the registered anchored-hour scale, because the empirical anchor is
weak ($R^2=0.018$); both scales are generated by the same linear transformation
and yield identical statistical inference.

Post-registration analyses include the measured screen-time models, spending
outcomes, detailed account-coverage checks, flexible age adjustment, and
diagnostic analyses of the calls item. These analyses are labelled exploratory
and are used to characterize and probe the observed association, not to
redefine the registered hypotheses. A table in
the Supplementary Information will classify every reported analysis as
originally registered, a post-registration correction, or exploratory and will
give the corresponding output file and specification. Full survey-item wording,
response scales, and the screen-time measurement protocol are given in the
Supplementary Information.
