\begin{abstract}
Digitalization holds the potential to reduce household carbon footprints by substituting for travel and material consumption, or increase them by facilitating and stimulating additional consumption. Evidence on the net individual-level relationship remains scarce. We link survey measures of digital
practices among Swedish adults to annual consumption-based greenhouse-gas
emissions estimated from bank transactions and to sociodemographic registers.
The complete-case analysis includes 4,250 participants. Our revised digital
lifestyle index combines 11 device-use and digital-activity frequencies. After adjustment for age, gender, income, education, household composition, and urbanity, a one-standard-deviation higher digital lifestyle index is associated with 204 kg CO\textsubscript{2}e more per year (95\% CI: 141--267), or 4.5\% of mean annual emissions. The gradient is distributed across multiple consumption categories and is accompanied by greater observed expenditure.
Contrary to a digital-substitution account, transport emissions and both at-home
and out-of-home leisure are non-negative among more digitally engaged
participants. Device-assisted mobile screen time is only weakly correlated with
the broader index and has no independent association with emissions. The results
identify digital engagement as a marker of consumption volume rather than
evidence that additional screen time itself causes emissions.
\end{abstract}

Yet the same technologies can lower the effort required to search for,
advertise, purchase, and deliver goods and services, while creating new
activities rather than replacing existing ones. Digitalization can therefore
reduce the environmental burden of a particular task without reducing the
overall level of consumption associated with a person's way of life.

Research on the environmental effects of information and communication
technologies (ICT) distinguishes the direct footprint of devices and digital
infrastructure from wider changes in production and consumption. Reviews find
that these indirect and higher-order effects are difficult to delimit and can
either offset or amplify direct efficiency gains \citep{horner2016,bieser2018,
pohl2019}. At the macro level, efficiency and sectoral change may reduce energy
requirements, while the production of ICT equipment, productivity-led growth,
and rebound effects work in the opposite direction \citep{lange2020}. The net
effect consequently depends on how technologies are adopted and how people use
the time, money, and opportunities they create.

Evidence from specific applications illustrates this ambiguity. The long-
standing expectation that telecommunications substitute for physical travel
has been qualified by evidence that communication and mobility can also
complement one another \citep{mokhtarian2002,andreev2010,aguilera2012}. A
systematic review of teleworking found that reduced commuting can save energy,
but that wider-boundary studies generally find smaller gains once home energy
use and non-work travel are considered \citep{hook2020}. Similarly, whether
online shopping has lower emissions than store-based shopping depends on
delivery organization, travel mode, trip chaining, basket size, and returns
\citep{wiese2012,shahmohammadi2020}. These studies show why the footprint of an
isolated digital service cannot establish whether more digitally engaged people
consume less overall. %I do not like the word engegaed here  but I cannot come up with something better either, accustomed maybe? 

An everyday-life perspective offers a different starting point. Digital tools
are not merely substitutes for analogue equivalents; they become woven into
bundles of work, social relations, entertainment, mobility, and consumption
\citep{ropke2012}. A systematic review of indirect ICT effects identified
consumer behaviour, social practices, and the dynamic consequences of adoption
as important research gaps \citep{bieser2018}. This perspective also changes the
interpretation of individual differences. Frequent calls, information searches,
or social-media use may facilitate consumption, but they may also identify
people who are more socially active, professionally connected, affluent, or
otherwise engaged in a larger number of activities that digital tools enable. Digital engagement may thus operate as a medium within a consumption-intensive lifestyle rather than as an independent cause of that lifestyle.

Some digital practices nevertheless provide plausible pathways to additional
consumption. Social-media platforms combine advertising, peer comparison, and
self-presentation. Existing studies relate social-media use to social
comparison and lower self-evaluations \citep{vogel2014,verduyn2017}, to envy and
conspicuous consumption \citep{taylor2016}, and to impulse fashion purchases
\citep{djafarova2021}. This literature makes a demand-stimulation mechanism
plausible, but much of the evidence concerns self-reported attitudes, purchase
intentions, or particular products. It does not establish whether social-media
engagement predicts total observed consumption; reverse causation and common lifestyle determinants remain equally possible.

Household carbon footprints rise strongly with affluence, making socioeconomic
adjustment essential when studying digital lifestyles \citep{ivanova2016,
oswald2020,wiedmann2020}. At the same time, conventional expenditure surveys and
application-specific life-cycle studies offer limited visibility into the
breadth of an individual's consumption. Financial-transaction data can provide
a more comprehensive, behaviorally observed account of expenditure and its
associated emissions \citep{andersson2020}. Linking such data to survey measures
of digital practices and administrative registers creates an opportunity to
study digital engagement as part of an observed consumption system rather than
as an isolated technology use case.

We use linked survey, bank-transaction, and register data from Swedish adults to
ask three questions. First, how is engagement across common digital practices
associated with total consumption-based greenhouse-gas emissions after
socioeconomic adjustment? Second, is the gradient concentrated in transport,
goods commonly purchased online, or digital services, or is it distributed
across consumption categories? Third, do measured mobile screen time and
reported leisure activities support a substitution account?

\section{Data}

\subsection{Sample}
We use a dataset that links surveys, bank transactions, user-profile information, and Swedish administrative registers at the individual level. Participants connected their bank account(s) to a research application, allowing historical transactions to be observed and categorized into consumption categories. The baseline survey measured device use and digital activities; a subsequent endline survey collected device-assisted mobile screen time and activity frequencies. All analyses of identifiable register-linked data were conducted in University of Gothenburg's Trusted Research Environment.

The baseline survey contains 12,153 unique participants. Of these participants,
11,172 satisfy the digital-battery response rule described below. We define an
observed transaction month as one in which non-excluded spending exceeds SEK
3,000 and require at least three such months during the 12 months preceding the
baseline survey. This restriction leaves 4,392 participants. Linkage to the
control variables leaves 4,251 participants, of whom 4,250 have complete data
for the fully adjusted model. We freeze this complete-case sample for all steps
of the main model sequence so that changes between specifications do not reflect
changes in sample composition.

\subsection{Consumption-based carbon footprints}
Transactions are assigned to consumption categories and converted to kilograms of carbon-dioxide
equivalents (CO\textsubscript{2}e) using category-specific emissions factors (Cite: Andersson Novel 2020).
We aggregate transactions by participant, month, and category. For each
participant--category combination observed in at least three valid months,
annual emissions are calculated as the sum over observed months divided by the
number of observed months and multiplied by 12. Annual emissions are winsorized
at the category-specific 99th percentile before categories are summed. The
primary outcome is total annual consumption-based CO\textsubscript{2}e.

We decompose emissions into theoretically motivated groups. Transport comprises fuel, vehicle maintenance and rental, public and
other ground transport, aviation, and ferry travel. The e-commerce-intensive
goods group comprises clothing, electronics, books, toys, sports goods, other
shopping, and home-and-garden goods; it denotes categories commonly purchased
online rather than transactions known to have occurred online. Internet and
telecommunications form the digital-services category. Insurance is used as a
low-channel placebo. We also retain mutually exclusive leaf categories to show
where the total gradient is distributed. Spending outcomes are constructed from
the corresponding transaction amounts using the same observation window and
annualization rule.

\subsection{Digital lifestyle intensity}
The baseline survey asks how frequently participants used five devices
(computer, smartphone, television, game console, and tablet) and performed
eight digital activities (streaming, phone or video calls, social media,
information search, gaming, digital reading, e-commerce, and online selling)
during the preceding three months. All items use a six-point response scale
ranging from no use to several times per hour.

The pre-registration specified an equally weighted index containing all 13
items. After registration, we recognized that e-commerce and online selling
measure the purchase channel itself and are therefore conceptually too proximal
to transaction-observed consumption outcomes. We treat their inclusion as a
design mistake rather than redefine the registered specification
retrospectively. The substantively preferred index consequently excludes these
two items and contains 11 items; the registered 13-item index is retained as a
sensitivity analysis.

Each item is standardized using its mean and standard deviation in the frozen
analysis sample. The index is the unweighted mean of the answered standardized
items and is itself re-standardized to mean zero and standard deviation one.
Following the pre-registered tolerance of up to three missing items, the revised
index requires at least eight of 11 responses. The revised index has Cronbach's $\alpha=0.56$. We interpret it as a formative composite of heterogeneous digital practices rather than as a reflective scale whose items are expected to be interchangeable.

At endline, respondents were instructed to read their average daily mobile
screen time for the previous week from iOS Screen Time or Android Digital
Wellbeing and transcribe hours and minutes. Reports above 18 hours per day are
treated as implausible. There are 2,200 valid reports, of which 2,082 can be
linked to a valid revised index. We regress measured hours on the standardized
index to obtain an interpretive anchor. The association is weak
($b=0.338$ hours per index SD, $R^2=0.018$), so kilograms per index SD is our
primary scale. Values per anchored hour are secondary and should not be read as
effects of an additional measured screen-time hour. Missing screen-time reports
do not exclude participants from analyses using the baseline index.

\subsection{Register variables}
The control variables comprise of demographic, application-profile, transaction, and register information. It includes age; gender; log income; log disposable register income; log mean monthly bank inflows;
educational attainment in five levels; household size; an indicator for
children; log population density in the participant's DeSO area; and an
indicator for residence in Stockholm, Gothenburg, or Malm\"o. One participant
is excluded because bank-income data are missing. Exact age enters linearly in
the main specification; a four-degree-of-freedom natural spline assesses
sensitivity to functional form.

\section{Empirical strategy}

\subsection{Net gradient}
We estimate cross-sectional ordinary least-squares models of the form
\begin{equation}
	Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \boldsymbol{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i,
	\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
where $Y_i$ is annual consumption-based CO\textsubscript{2}e, $D_i$ is the
standardized digital lifestyle index, and $\boldsymbol{X}_i$ is the adjustment
set. The coefficient $\beta$ is reported as kilograms of annual
CO\textsubscript{2}e per index standard deviation. We use HC3
heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors and two-sided tests with 95\%
confidence intervals.

Controls are introduced sequentially on the same complete-case sample: M0 is
bivariate; M1 adds gender and age; M2 adds the three income measures and
education; M3 adds household size and children; M4 adds log population density;
and M5 adds major-city residence. M5 is the primary adjusted specification.
These models estimate conditional gradients, not causal effects: digital
practices are not randomly assigned, and unmeasured preferences, employment,
or life circumstances may influence both digital behavior and consumption.

\subsection{Decomposition}
We replace total emissions in Equation~\ref{eq:main} with each grouped and leaf
category while retaining the M5 adjustment set. The transport coefficient tests
the proposed demobilization margin; e-commerce-intensive goods and digital
services capture material and direct digital channels. Because grouped and leaf
categories overlap, grouped estimates are interpreted separately rather than
summed. The mutually exclusive leaf estimates provide the additive description
of the total gradient. Insurance serves as a low-channel falsification outcome,
although low mean emissions limit the precision of formal equivalence tests.

To examine whether the emissions gradient coincides with greater consumption,
we additionally estimate M5 models for annual emission-relevant spending and
total observed spending. These spending analyses are descriptive and are not
interpreted as causal mediation.

\subsection{Mechanism}
The pre-registered mechanism proposed that digital engagement substitutes for
out-of-home activity and thereby reduces transport. The endline survey records
12-month frequencies on seven-point scales for seven out-of-home activities
(library visits, sports and cultural events, hosting dinner guests, dancing,
exercise, and time in nature) and five primarily at-home activities
(audiobooks, music, books, films, and television series). Because hosting dinner
guests, exercise, and time in nature do not invariably entail travel or paid
consumption, we refer to the first measure more precisely as the
out-of-home/social-activity composite. We form unweighted means of the available
items within each domain.

We first regress each activity and both composites on the digital index and M5
controls. We then estimate the transport and total-emissions gradients on one
common endline subsample: without the leisure composites, adding out-of-home
leisure, and adding both composites. The percentage attenuation of $\beta$ is
reported as the share of the gradient statistically accounted for by these
measures. Because exposure, leisure, and emissions are not jointly randomized
or temporally isolated, this is a descriptive mechanism check rather than a
causal mediation analysis.

\subsection{Heterogeneity}
We report M5-adjusted index gradients by pre-specified age group
(18--29, 30--44, 45--64, and 65+), gender, and major-city residence. Formal
tests add the corresponding index-by-group interaction to M5; differences
between subgroup point estimates are not inferred from separate confidence
intervals. Income-quintile patterns are exploratory.

\subsection{Design caveats}
The baseline frequency battery precedes the endline screen-time report, while
transaction outcomes are constructed from the 12 months preceding the baseline
survey. The anchor therefore maps a broader, earlier digital-practice composite
onto a one-week measure of later mobile screen time; it does not establish that
the two constructs are equivalent. We accordingly separate the main digital
lifestyle analysis from models using measured screen time itself.

Observed spending may also depend on how completely participants connected
their bank accounts. We assess this by adding account-count controls, estimating
models within account-count strata, normalizing spending and emissions by
register income, and restricting the sample to participants whose observed
spending is within pre-defined proportions of register income. Further
sensitivity analyses use the registered 13-item index, the index excluding
calls, device and activity subindices, a first principal component, a nonlinear
age adjustment, DeSO fixed effects, stricter transaction coverage, alternative
housing and life-stage controls, and the E4-reporting subsample. These checks
probe measurement and specification dependence but do not remove the
cross-sectional design's fundamental risk of residual confounding and reverse
causation.

\subsection{Decomposition across consumption categories}
The emissions gradient was distributed across multiple consumption categories.
Transport emissions increased by $46$ kg CO\textsubscript{2}e per index SD

mechanism. Emissions from the e-commerce-intensive goods group increased by
$28$ kg (95\% CI: $18$--$39$; $p<0.001$), and internet and telecommunications

outcome was small and not statistically distinguishable from zero ($0.14$ kg;
$p=0.126$).

($22$ kg), travel ($21$ kg), uncategorized purchases ($17$ kg), clothing
($11$ kg), other shopping ($8$ kg), and alcohol ($8$ kg) per index SD. The
pattern was therefore broad rather than confined to purchases of digital

\subsection{Mechanism: out-of-home leisure}
More digitally engaged participants did not report less out-of-home or social
activity. The adjusted out-of-home/social-activity composite increased by 0.073
points per index SD (95\% CI: 0.037--0.109; $p<0.001$), while the at-home
composite increased by 0.137 points (95\% CI: 0.101--0.172; $p<0.001$).
Positive associations were observed for attending sports events (0.164 points),
attending cultural events (0.088), hosting dinner guests (0.137), and dancing
(0.144), as well as for audiobooks, music, films, and television series.

On the common mechanism subsample ($N=2,392$), the M5 transport gradient was
$62.7$ kg CO\textsubscript{2}e per SD. Adding the out-of-home/social-activity
composite reduced it to $61.6$ kg, and adding both activity composites reduced
it to $56.7$ kg, an attenuation of 9.6\%. For total emissions, the corresponding
estimates were $237.8$, $227.4$, and $213.2$ kg per SD, an attenuation of
10.3\% after both composites. These measures therefore accounted for only a
limited share of the observed gradients and did not support substitution away
from out-of-home activity.

\subsection{Heterogeneity}
Adjusted subgroup estimates were positive for participants aged 18--29

\section{Discussion}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Principal interpretation.} Greater engagement across digital
		practices was associated with higher consumption-based emissions and higher
		observed expenditure. The association was distributed across several
		consumption categories and remained positive under alternative index
		constructions. In this sample, digital engagement therefore did not mark a
		dematerialized lifestyle.

	\item \textbf{Digitalization as medium rather than isolated exposure.} The
		results should not be interpreted as showing that additional screen time
		causes emissions. Device-assisted mobile screen time was only weakly related
		to the broader practice composite and had no independent association with
		emissions. Calls---the item with the largest gradient---can represent both
		private communication and occupational video meetings. A plausible reading
		is that digital tools are embedded in socially and economically active
		lives, facilitating some activities while also serving as markers of them.

	\item \textbf{Limited support for substitution.} More digitally engaged
		participants did not report less out-of-home activity, and their transport
		emissions were higher rather than lower. Adding the leisure measures
		attenuated the transport and total-emissions gradients only modestly. This
		accords with literature showing that telecommunications may complement
		mobility and that energy savings from telework shrink when wider behavioral
		responses are included \citep{mokhtarian2002,hook2020}. The result does not
		imply that no digital application can substitute for travel; it shows that
		such substitution was not visible in the aggregate lifestyle pattern.

	\item \textbf{Heterogeneous digital practices.} The low internal consistency
		of the index and the contrasting item coefficients argue against a single
		latent ``digital intensity'' trait. Social media retained a positive
		association conditional on the other practices, while streaming retained a
		negative one. Social comparison, advertising, and self-presentation offer a
		plausible interpretation of the social-media result
		\citep{vogel2014,taylor2016,djafarova2021}, but the present data do not
		measure envy, advertising exposure, or compensatory motives. Category-level
		social-media and spending analyses are needed before claiming a demand-
		stimulation mechanism.

	\item \textbf{Measurement and alternative explanations.} Residual
		socioeconomic differences, employment intensity, social activity, and bank-
		account coverage may influence both digital practices and observed
		consumption. Controlling for the number of connected accounts reduced, but
		did not remove, the main gradient. Restrictions based on spending relative
		to register income are less decisive because they may select on genuine
		saving, borrowing, and household financial arrangements as well as account
		coverage. These checks should therefore qualify the magnitude rather than be
		treated as corrections that identify a causal effect.

	\item \textbf{Contribution to environmental assessment.} Most assessments of
		ICT compare particular digital and non-digital services, while reviews call
		for greater attention to consumer behavior and social practices
		\citep{horner2016,bieser2018,pohl2019}. Linking practice measures to broad
		transaction-derived footprints complements those studies by showing the
		consumption context in which digital technologies are used. The approach is
		especially useful for detecting activity that is displaced, added, or
		reorganized outside the boundary of a single service comparison.

	\item \textbf{Implications for scenarios and policy.} Prospective assessments
		should not represent digitalization solely as a technical substitution with
		a fixed avoided activity. Scenarios should also vary behavioral responses:
		whether digital services replace existing consumption, complement it, or
		expand the range and frequency of activities. Policy claims about enabling
		effects should therefore state the assumed baseline, rebound mechanisms, and
		conditions under which savings persist.

	\item \textbf{Scope and future work.} The study is observational, the digital
		battery measures frequency rather than duration, transactions precede the
		endline measures, and participants are a selected group willing to connect
		bank accounts. Future work should combine longitudinal changes in digital
		practices with transaction outcomes, distinguish occupational from private
		communication, measure social and advertising exposure directly, and test
		whether changes in specific practices precede changes in spending. Such
		designs are needed to distinguish digital tools as causes, mediators, and
		markers of consumption-intensive lifestyles.
\end{itemize}

After the first disclosure-controlled aggregate outputs had been inspected, a
linked addendum documented the analyses already conducted and specified further
item-level, screen-time, account-coverage, age-flexibility, telework, and

addendum are distinguished from those in the original registration; analyses

The principal deviation concerns exposure construction. The registered index
included e-commerce and online selling. We subsequently concluded that this was
a design mistake because these items directly measure participation in purchase
channels while the outcomes are transaction-observed consumption. We therefore
use the 11-item index excluding those two items as the substantively preferred,
post-registration correction and retain the registered 13-item estimate in all
index-sensitivity results. The associated response threshold changes from at
least 10 of 13 to at least eight of 11 items, preserving the registered
tolerance of three missing responses. We also make the index-SD scale primary,
rather than the registered anchored-hour scale, because the empirical anchor is
weak ($R^2=0.018$); both scales are generated by the same linear transformation
and yield identical statistical inference.

Other post-registration analyses include the spending outcomes, detailed
account-count restrictions, and diagnostic analyses of the calls item. These
are used to characterize and probe the observed association, not to redefine
the registered hypotheses. A table in
the Supplementary Information will classify every reported analysis as
originally registered, addendum-specified, post-registration correction, or
exploratory and will give the corresponding output file and specification.

The calls item combines ordinary phone calls and video calls in one response.
It therefore cannot distinguish occupational video meetings from private
communication. Analyses of this item are interpreted as exploratory diagnostics
of a mixed communication measure.

\subsection{Device-assisted mobile screen time}
The endline item asks respondents how much time they spend using their mobile
phone and instructs them to open iOS ``Screen Time'' or Android ``Digital
Wellbeing'' and report the displayed average daily use for the previous week.
Hours and minutes are entered in separate fields. Respondents can instead select
``I have not activated that function'' or ``Skip the question/I cannot find the
information.'' We convert the two fields to decimal hours per day and exclude
values above 18 hours. The survey does not record operating system, and the item
measures mobile-device use rather than total time across all digital devices.

\subsection{Endline activity battery}
The endline battery asks, ``How often during the past 12 months have you done
the following?'' Responses range from (1) never, through (2) once during the
past 12 months, (3) once every six months, (4) once per quarter, (5) once per
month, and (6) once per week, to (7) several times per week.

The out-of-home/social-activity composite contains visiting a library
(F88\_4), attending a sports event (F88\_5), attending a cultural event such as
an art exhibition, theatre performance, or concert (F88\_6), inviting someone
home for dinner (F88\_7), dancing (F88\_8), exercising (F88\_18), and spending
time in nature or outdoor recreation (F88\_21). The at-home composite contains
listening to an audiobook (F88\_11), listening to music (F88\_12), reading a
book (F88\_13), watching a film (F88\_14), and watching a television series
(F88\_16). The domain labels are analytical groupings: individual activities,
especially exercise and reading, need not occur exclusively inside or outside
the home.
