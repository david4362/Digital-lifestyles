\section{Study information}

\subsection{Research questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Net gradient.} What is the association between digital lifestyle
    intensity, expressed per
    additional daily hour of screen time, and total consumption-based CO\textsubscript{2}e controlling for sociodemographic factors?
  \item \textbf{Decomposition.} Is the gradient negative in transport  (demobilization), social spending categories, 
   and positive in goods, e-commerce-intensive categories,
    and digital services (rebound and direct effects), with no-channel categories (rent, insurance) as placebos?
  \item \textbf{Mechanism.} Does lower out-of-home leisure frequency, measured through self-stated survey items and specific consumption activities, among the digitally intensive account for part of the transport gradient?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\paragraph{RQ1: Net gradient.} We register \emph{no} directional hypothesis for
the association between digital lifestyle intensity and total consumption-based
CO\textsubscript{2}e. The two competing accounts in the literature
(dematerialization/demobilization vs.\ rebound and digitally induced demand)
predict opposite signs, and adjudicating between them is the purpose of the
study. The test is two-sided.

\paragraph{RQ2: Decomposition.} The composition of the gradient, rather than its
net sign, carries the directional predictions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[H2a.] The gradient in \emph{transport} CO\textsubscript{2}e is
    \emph{negative} (demobilization: digital time use substitutes for physical
    mobility).
  \item[H2b.] The gradients in \emph{goods}, \emph{e-commerce-intensive
    categories}, and \emph{digital services} are \emph{positive} (rebound of
    freed time and money into consumption; direct demand generation through
    digital channels; direct spending on digital services).
  \item[H2c.] \emph{Placebo:} the gradients in \emph{rent} and \emph{insurance}
    are \emph{null}. These categories have no plausible digital substitution or
    demand channel; a non-null gradient here would indicate residual confounding
    (e.g., by income or household composition) rather than a digital-lifestyle
    effect, and would qualify the causal interpretation of H2a--H2b. H2c is
    tested by equivalence (TOST), not by absence of significance: support for
    H2c requires rejecting both one-sided tests against the equivalence bounds
    specified in Section~\ref{sec:inference}.
\end{itemize}
Jointly, the signature pattern H2a--H2c (negative transport, positive
goods/e-commerce/digital services, null placebos) is the pre-registered evidence
that both substitution and rebound channels operate; observing only one side
would favor the corresponding account.

\paragraph{RQ3: Mechanism.} Two linked predictions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[H3a.] Digital lifestyle intensity is \emph{negatively} associated with
    out-of-home leisure frequency (endline E1).
  \item[H3b.] Adjusting for out-of-home leisure frequency \emph{attenuates} the
    transport gradient (H2a); we report the share of the gradient accounted for,
    without claiming a causal mediation estimate.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Heterogeneity (pre-specified, non-directional).} Moderation of the
net gradient and the transport gradient by age, gender, and urbanity is
pre-specified but registered without directional priors; these analyses are
labeled exploratory in the paper.

\section{Data description}

This study reuses existing data (the Konsumtionskollen sample): approximately
6{,}000 Swedish adults who completed surveys in 2024 and connected their bank
accounts to a research app that retrieved transactions back to 2019, categorized
them to COICOP, and converted them to CO\textsubscript{2}e. Register linkage
provides income, education, household composition, and DeSO-level urbanity.
Analyses of real data take place in the SCB Trusted Research Environment (TRE).

\paragraph{Prior data access.} This is a secondary analysis of existing data,
and members of the team have worked with the dataset before; we disclose the
full extent of prior access.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Previous publication.} The team has previously analyzed the
    Konsumtionskollen data for a separate study \todo{cite the first paper +
    link to its analysis repository}. That work used the consumption-based
    CO\textsubscript{2}e outcomes, spending data, register covariates, and
    baseline survey items on environmental self-identity. \textbf{It did not
    analyze the exposure variables of the present study}: the device-use and
    digital-activity frequency batteries (baseline q11/q11b) and the
    device-assisted screen-time report (endline E4) have not been related to
    emissions or spending in any prior analysis by the team.
  \item \emph{Prior descriptive knowledge.} From the previous work, the team
    knows the distributions of total and category-level CO\textsubscript{2}e,
    spending patterns, and covariate distributions in this sample. The
    hypotheses in this document concern gradients with respect to digital
    lifestyle intensity, which cannot be derived from that knowledge.
  \item \emph{Code development.} All analysis code for the present study was
    developed against a de-identified development extract of the data in which
    values are partly scrambled. Descriptive distributions of baseline survey
    items (including the q11/q11b batteries) were examined in this extract to
    identify and validate variable definitions, but no digital-lifestyle
    variable was related to emissions or spending. The endline survey,
    including E4, is absent from the extract and has not been accessed by the
    team in any form beyond the questionnaire document; development of the
    E4-dependent code used fully synthetic endline data generated by the team.
  \item \emph{Real-data access.} No analysis relating digital-lifestyle
    variables to consumption or emissions has been run on real data. Real-data
    analysis in the SCB Trusted Research Environment begins only after this
    pre-registration is registered.
\end{itemize}

\section{Variables}

\subsection{Exposure: digital lifestyle intensity index}
A pre-registered index combining:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Device-use frequencies} (baseline q11\_1--q11\_5+; 1--6 scale):
    computer, smartphone, TV, game console, tablet.
  \item \textbf{Digital-activity frequencies} (baseline q11b\_1--q11b\_8; 1--6
    scale): streaming, calls, social media, information search, gaming, digital
    reading, e-commerce, online selling.
\end{itemize}
The index is anchored cardinally by the device-assisted screen-time report
(endline E4: average daily screen time in hours:minutes for the previous week,
read from iOS Sk\"armtid / Android Digitalt v\"alm\aa ende).

\paragraph{Aggregation.} Each of the 13 items (5 device-use + 8
digital-activity) is standardized to a z-score using the analysis-sample mean
and SD. The raw index is the unweighted mean of non-missing standardized items,
computed for respondents answering at least 10 of 13 items; it is then
re-standardized (mean 0, SD 1). Equal weighting is the primary specification;
alternative weightings (first principal component; device-use and
digital-activity halves separately) are registered as robustness checks.

\paragraph{Cardinal anchoring.} Among respondents with a valid E4 report, we
estimate the OLS anchoring regression
\begin{equation*}
  \text{E4}_i \;(\text{hours/day}) = a + b \, z_i + u_i,
\end{equation*}
where $z_i$ is the standardized index. The anchored index is the fitted value
$\hat{a} + \hat{b} z_i$, defined for \emph{all} respondents with a valid index
(including E4 opt-outs), expressed in daily screen-time hours. All gradients are
reported per additional daily hour on this scale; per-SD estimates on the
unanchored index are reported alongside, so the anchoring affects
interpretability, not inference. E4 responses are parsed from hours:minutes to
decimal hours; values above 18 hours/day are set to missing as implausible.
We report $\hat{b}$, its confidence interval, and the $R^2$ of the anchoring
regression; a weak anchor (low $R^2$) is a registered caveat against the
cardinal interpretation, not a gate on the analysis.

\paragraph{Reliability.} We report Cronbach's $\alpha$ for the full 13-item
index and for each half (device-use, digital-activity), plus item--rest
correlations. No $\alpha$ threshold gates the analysis; low reliability is
addressed by the registered robustness checks (halves separately, principal
component).

\paragraph{E4 opt-outs.} E4 offers ``function not activated'' and ``prefer not
to answer'' options; missingness is expected. Opt-outs are excluded from the
anchoring regression only --- they retain their anchored index value and remain
in all main analyses. We report the opt-out share and compare opt-outs to
reporters on the index and covariates as a selection check. As robustness, main
models are re-estimated on the E4-reporting subsample.

\subsection{Inference criteria}
\label{sec:inference}
All directional and non-directional hypotheses (RQ1, H2a--H2b, H3a--H3b) are
tested two-sided at $\alpha = 0.05$ with HC3 robust standard errors.

\paragraph{Equivalence testing for the placebo hypothesis (H2c).} The null
gradients in rent and insurance are assessed with two one-sided tests (TOST) at
$\alpha = 0.05$ per one-sided test. The smallest effect size of interest is set
\emph{relative to the category mean}: equivalence bounds of
$\pm 1\%$ of the sample-mean annual CO\textsubscript{2}e in the placebo
category per additional daily hour of screen time. H2c is supported for a
category if the 90\% confidence interval for the gradient lies entirely within
the bounds; a gradient outside the bounds (conventional test significant at
$\alpha = 0.05$) counts as placebo failure; an inconclusive TOST (CI straddling
a bound) is reported as such. \todo{Confirm the $\pm 1\%$ SESOI --- benchmark
