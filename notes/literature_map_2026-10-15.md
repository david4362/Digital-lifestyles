# Literature map for the digital lifestyles manuscript

This is a curated map, not a claim to exhaustive coverage. Metadata and DOIs
were verified through Crossref. The central open-access review claims were also
checked against abstracts or full text. Closed or abstract-only sources are
marked for coauthor verification before submission.

## Core framing: indirect and higher-order effects

- **Horner, Shehabi & Azevedo (2016), ERL.** "Known unknowns: indirect energy
  effects of information and communication technology."
  DOI: 10.1088/1748-9326/11/10/103001. Core source for uncertain net effects,
  sensitivity to boundaries, and the importance of deployment and user
  behaviour. Abstract/full record accessible.
- **Bieser & Hilty (2018), Sustainability.** "Assessing Indirect Environmental
  Effects of ICT: A Systematic Literature Review."
  DOI: 10.3390/su10082662. Most useful gap statement: assessments focus more on
  production than consumption and need research on consumer behaviour, social
  practices, and dynamic change. Open access.
- **Pohl, Hilty & Finkbeiner (2019), Journal of Cleaner Production.** "How LCA
  contributes to the environmental assessment of higher order effects of ICT
  application."
  DOI: 10.1016/j.jclepro.2019.02.018. Supports the distinction between service-
  level comparisons and wider higher-order effects. Detailed abstract/metadata
  accessible; full text should be checked before making method-specific claims.
- **Lange, Pohl & Santarius (2020), Ecological Economics.** "Digitalization and
  energy consumption. Does ICT reduce energy demand?"
  DOI: 10.1016/j.ecolecon.2020.106760. Provides the direct, efficiency, growth,
  and sectoral-change framework and argues that increasing effects may dominate.
  Abstract accessible.
- **Ropke & Christensen (2012), Telematics and Informatics.** "Energy impacts
  of ICT: Insights from an everyday life perspective."
  DOI: 10.1016/j.tele.2012.02.001. Conceptually central for treating ICT as
  embedded in practices rather than isolated services. **Abstract/full text not
  accessible in the current search; verify the manuscript's characterization
  against the PDF.**

## Substitution, complementarity, and rebound

- **Mokhtarian (2002), Journal of Industrial Ecology.** "Telecommunications and
  Travel: The Case for Complementarity."
  DOI: 10.1162/108819802763471771. Foundational challenge to simple travel
  substitution. **Full text not accessed; verify detailed use.**
- **Andreev, Salomon & Pliskin (2010), Transportation Research C.** "Review:
  State of teleactivities." DOI: 10.1016/j.trc.2009.04.017. Review context for
  telework, teleshopping, and other teleactivities.
- **Aguilera, Guillot & Rallet (2012), Transportation Research A.** "Mobile
  ICTs and physical mobility: Review and research agenda."
  DOI: 10.1016/j.tra.2012.01.005. Supports context-dependent complementarity
  between communication and mobility. **Full text not accessed.**
- **Hook et al. (2020), ERL.** "A systematic review of the energy and climate
  impacts of teleworking." DOI: 10.1088/1748-9326/ab8a84. Strong review evidence
  that commuting savings can be offset by home energy and non-work travel;
  wider-boundary studies find smaller savings. Open access.

## E-commerce and retail boundaries

- **Wiese, Toporowski & Zielke (2012), Transportation Research D.** "Transport-
  related CO2 effects of online and brick-and-mortar shopping."
  DOI: 10.1016/j.trd.2012.05.007. Demonstrates sensitivity to consumer travel
  and delivery assumptions in clothing retail. **Full text not accessed.**
- **Shahmohammadi et al. (2020), Environmental Science & Technology.**
  "Comparative Greenhouse Gas Footprinting of Online versus Traditional
  Shopping for Fast-Moving Consumer Goods."
  DOI: 10.1021/acs.est.9b06252. Stochastic comparison emphasizing dependence on
  delivery and consumer behaviour. Metadata verified; verify exact findings
  against full text before using quantitative claims.

## Social media and possible demand stimulation

- **Vogel et al. (2014), Psychology of Popular Media Culture.** "Social
  comparison, social media, and self-esteem."
  DOI: 10.1037/ppm0000047. Supports the social-comparison pathway. **Full text
  not accessed.**
- **Verduyn et al. (2017), Social Issues and Policy Review.** "Do Social Network
  Sites Enhance or Undermine Subjective Well-Being?"
  DOI: 10.1111/sipr.12033. Critical review distinguishing types of use and
  well-being pathways. **Full text not accessed.**
- **Taylor & Strutton (2016), Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing.**
  "Does Facebook usage lead to conspicuous consumption? The role of envy,
  narcissism and self-promotion."
  DOI: 10.1108/JRIM-01-2015-0009. Directly relevant, but **abstract/full text was
  not accessible; treat as provisional until checked.**
- **Djafarova & Bowes (2021), Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services.**
  "Instagram made me buy it: Generation Z impulse purchases in fashion
  industry." DOI: 10.1016/j.jretconser.2020.102345. Qualitative focus-group
  evidence on Instagram marketing and impulse fashion purchases, especially
  among women. Abstract accessible; narrow population and outcome limit
  generalization.

## Consumption footprints, affluence, and method

- **Andersson (2020), Journal of Cleaner Production.** "A novel approach to
  calculate individuals' carbon footprints using financial transaction data."
  DOI: 10.1016/j.jclepro.2020.120396. Direct methodological foundation for the
  transaction-derived footprint data. Abstract accessible.
- **Ivanova et al. (2016), Journal of Industrial Ecology.** "Environmental
  Impact Assessment of Household Consumption."
  DOI: 10.1111/jiec.12371. Broad household-footprint and consumption-category
  context.
- **Oswald, Owen & Steinberger (2020), Nature Energy.** "Large inequality in
  international and intranational energy footprints between income groups and
  across consumption categories." DOI: 10.1038/s41560-020-0579-8. Supports
  socioeconomic adjustment and affluence context.
- **Wiedmann et al. (2020), Nature Communications.** "Scientists' warning on
  affluence." DOI: 10.1038/s41467-020-16941-y. Supports the broader argument
  that technology alone does not offset consumption expansion. Open access.

## Relevant additions not yet cited

- **Court & Sorrell (2020), ERL.** "Digitalisation of goods: a systematic review
  of the determinants and magnitude of the impacts on energy consumption."
  DOI: 10.1088/1748-9326/ab6788. Strong candidate if the manuscript develops the
  virtual-goods/streaming result.
- **Santarius, Pohl & Lange (2020), Sustainability.** "Digitalization and the
  Decoupling Debate."
  DOI: 10.3390/su12187496. Useful for a more explicit decoupling framing, but
  overlaps with Lange et al. (2020).
- **Freitag et al. (2021), Patterns.** "The real climate and transformative
  impact of ICT: A critique of estimates, trends, and regulations."
  DOI: 10.1016/j.patter.2021.100340. Useful if the paper discusses the direct
  ICT footprint; currently peripheral to the individual-level contribution.

## Recommended full-text requests

Before submission, obtain and check Ropke & Christensen (2012), Mokhtarian
(2002), Aguilera et al. (2012), Taylor & Strutton (2016), Vogel et al. (2014),
Wiese et al. (2012), and Shahmohammadi et al. (2020). The current draft makes
only modest claims from these sources, but full-text verification is preferable.
